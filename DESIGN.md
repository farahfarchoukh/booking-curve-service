# DESIGN.md — Booking Curve Forecasting Service

## 6.1 One model or many?

**Global backbone + explicit per-hotel shrinkage — not per-hotel models, not raw hotel-identity as a feature.**

The data settles this. `hotel_C` has 1,463 trainable curves; `hotel_H` has 40 — essentially a cold-start case dressed up with a few off-season rows. A per-hotel model for `hotel_H` has nothing to fit, and at hundreds-of-hotels scale most new tenants will look like `hotel_H`. A naive global model with `hotel_id` as a feature is worse than it looks: it can only special-case hotels it has seen, degrading to the population mean for `hotel_A/D/E/F/G` and every hotel onboarded tomorrow — exactly backwards, since those most need the shared model to generalize.

So `src/features.py` never exposes `hotel_id` or `room_type_code` to the trees — only what's known at onboarding, before a single reservation exists: `country`, `currency`, `pms_type`, `primary_rate_mode`, `region_type`, room-count scale (`total_rooms`, `inventory_count`, `room_share`), `room_type_kind`, plus calendar. This "backbone" must already produce something sane for a hotel with zero history.

On top of it, an empirical-Bayes (James–Stein) per-hotel bias correction: `correction[h] = (n_h/(n_h+K)) · mean(OOF residual for h)`, computed separately for the level and shape stages. `hotel_C` (n=1,463, K=15) gets ~99% of its own signal back; `hotel_H` (n=40) gets ~73%, correctly distrusting its thin, off-season sample; a brand-new hotel (n=0) gets exactly the backbone. This is a hierarchical model's *behavior* — shrink to the population as n shrinks — without fitting one, chosen because with 2 hotels there's nothing to estimate a group-level variance from. I decomposed the target into **level** (final occupancy, cp=0) and **shape** (fraction of final occupancy realized by each cp, monotonic in cp) so shrinkage acts on one clean scalar per curve instead of 10 correlated checkpoints, and because level and shape transfer differently across seasons (§6.2). Caveat: with only 2 fitted hotels, `region_type`/`pms_type`/`total_rooms` get exactly zero split gain (`evaluation/feature_importance.py` — checked in, not eyeballed; an earlier draft of this paragraph guessed `primary_rate_mode` was one of the zero-gain three before I actually ran the numbers, and it isn't) — collinear with `country`/`currency`/`room_share` at this sample size. The mechanism is correctly wired, but I can't yet claim it learned the *right* attribute relationships from this sample. What the level model actually leans on: `doy_sin`/`doy_cos` (52% combined gain — calendar seasonality dominates, as it should), `room_share`/`inventory_count` (32% combined — room-type scale relative to the hotel), `dow` (9%). The shape model is dominated by `cp` itself (64% gain) — the right sanity check that "how many days until stay" is actually driving the pace curve, not an artifact of the decomposition.

This pooling actually runs two levels deep, not one — §6.8 covers the second level (within a hotel, across its room types) and the extrapolation-damping fix that same work forced.

**The level x shape decomposition itself was a first-principles call, unvalidated against the alternative, until `evaluation/architecture_comparison.py`.** The obvious simplest baseline is Approach A: one model, no decomposition, `cp` as an ordinal feature under the same monotone constraint. Backbone-only (no shrinkage on either side, isolating the decomposition question from the correction machinery built on top of it), trained and compared on the 3 rolling-backtest tuning folds that exclude the official split (same nested-CV discipline as §6.11's calibration search): the shipped decomposition wins, 0.241 vs. 0.252 weighted MAE (~4.3% better) — decisive at that granularity. On the single official Jul-Sep split alone the two are statistically indistinguishable (0.271 vs. 0.270, a 0.0009 gap that's well inside the ~0.01-0.02 noise band §6.9's seed-sensitivity check already established) — which doesn't contradict the tuning-fold result, it's exactly what "one split can be noisy" looks like, and it's why the tuning-fold comparison is the one to trust. Beyond the raw number, the decomposition earns its complexity independently: it's what makes "one clean scalar per curve" possible for the shrinkage mechanism above, a property Approach A doesn't have without shrinking 10 correlated per-checkpoint residuals separately — more parameters to estimate on a dataset already this small. Approaches C (a model per horizon) and D (multi-output) were ruled out before training either: C means 10 models each fit on ~1,463 curve-level rows instead of B's shared ~13,167 shape rows, with zero learning transfer between adjacent horizons, which contradicts the entire premise that a booking curve has structure; D has no native LightGBM implementation (`MultiOutputRegressor` is just C again) and a genuine multi-output tree/neural approach isn't justified by 1,503 level-stage curves. `evaluation/architecture_comparison.json` has the full numbers.

## 6.2 Cold start

Day 0: a new hotel has a room dimension table and nothing else. `predict_booking_curve` already handles this on the same code path as everything else — `hotel_n_obs` returns 0, shrinkage is 0, and the curve comes from the attribute backbone alone.

The harder truth this dataset surfaces directly: `hotel_H` is a mountain resort whose busy season (Jul–Sep) is **entirely unseen** in its own Apr–Jun training window, and no other resort-mountain property exists to borrow a season shape from. I measured this rather than assumed it: my level model's out-of-fold PICP is 90% in-distribution but only ~62% on the true test window (see §6.8 — it was 39% before I found and fixed a real bug this surfaced), with actuals still landing above p90 more often than below p10 — a one-sided miss consistent with real seasonal shift, not a calibration bug. Widening the interval (an extrapolation-distance term in `model.py`) only partly helps — a symmetric interval can't fully fix a biased median. I did not hand-correct the bias using knowledge of test outcomes; that would be fitting the answer key, not solving cold start.

What I'd ship instead: (1) a `season_calendar` reference table (peak/shoulder/low by month, from RM ops or a comp-set) as a real feature — its absence is *why* hotel_H is hard here; (2) borrow *shape* (not level) from the nearest `region_type` peers once ≥3 exist per type; (3) flag any prediction outside the hotel's observed day-of-year range as `low_confidence` so the pricer treats it conservatively rather than trusting the point estimate. Graduation: week 1 = pure backbone; month 1 = shrinkage engages as n passes ~15–40 curves; month 6, after a full seasonal cycle, a hotel's own weight dominates and the flag stops firing in-season.

## 6.3 Features and the data plane

| Feature | Anchor | Leakage risk | Freshness | Availability |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Calendar (dow, doy sin/cos, week) | `stay_date` | None | N/A | Universal |
| Hotel/room attributes | `hotels.csv`/`room_types.csv` | None (static) | Days | Universal |
| `cp` / days-until-stay | derived | None | N/A | Universal |
| Realized pickup-to-date | `reservations`, `as_of_date`-gated | **High** if the cutoff is wrong | Minutes | hotel_C/H here; any onboarded hotel in prod |

I excluded `daily_inventory`, `daily_hotel_demand`, `competitor_rates`, `suggested_prices`, and `expected_booking_curves` from training: every one covers *only* the Jul–Sep test window for hotel_B/C/F (e.g. `daily_inventory` starts 2025-09-08) — zero training-period rows to learn a coefficient from. Wiring them in wouldn't be classic leakage so much as an untestable relationship (populated at inference, NaN at training). The data dictionary's warning about `daily_inventory.actual_occupancy` — "at `as_of_date == stay_date` the snapshot *is* the cp=0 target" — is the canonical version of this bug: joining an operational snapshot at the wrong as-of instant. I catch this structurally: `realized_fraction_as_of()` is the *only* function allowed to read post-cutoff activity, takes an explicit `as_of_date`, and — no longer just a stated intention — `tests/test_leakage.py` is exactly that property test, asserting `predict_booking_curve(..., as_of_date=d)` is invariant to any reservation with `booking_date > d`, with a positive control proving the test would fail if that weren't true. It runs in CI on every push.

`expected_booking_curves` I use as an **evaluation baseline**, not a feature — it too only exists in the test window, so I can't validate a blend weight without peeking at the window I'm scored on. Computed continuously in production it becomes a legitimate always-available prior to stack with (inverse-error-weighted, fit on rolling backtests). `suggested_prices` is the pricer's *own past output*; using it as a demand feature closes a feedback loop (learns "pricer priced high → demand was X" instead of the cause) — useful for an off-policy analysis of the pricer, never as a forecasting feature. `competitor_rates`/`hotel_comp_set` is sparse and its room-type label doesn't map to ours — a coarse weekly market-position control at best, not a daily feature yet.

## 6.4 Multi-currency, multi-timezone, multi-PMS

`stay_date` is a calendar date **in the hotel's own local timezone** (`hotels.timezone`), never converted to UTC — a stay is "the night of Aug 15 at this hotel," and UTC midnight has no meaning there. PMS heterogeneity (`agora` vs `cms`) is a different data source with a shared target schema: `src/data.py` normalizes both into one `(hotel_id, room_type_code, stay_date, booking_date, checkout_date, status)` shape at ingestion; `pms_type` stays a feature because cancellation semantics and sync cadence still differ downstream.

I never touch price here — `rate_amount` is stripped, and every rate table only covers the test window, so ADR isn't trainable regardless of currency. If it were: never train on raw currency units. I'd want `fx_rates(currency, date, usd_rate)` (at least daily) and convert every rate to USD-equivalent *at the rate's own date* — using today's FX rate to normalize a March rate injects a macro trend into the target. `primary_rate_mode` (hotel_C's `average` vs. everyone else's `lowest`) is the same class of gotcha: two hotels' "rate" fields aren't comparable even in the same currency without knowing which aggregation produced them.

## 6.5 Training and serving infrastructure

Feature engineering lives in one module (`src/features.py`), imported by both `train.py` and `predict.py` — that's the actual guarantee against train/serve skew, not a policy. Serving is hybrid: nightly batch-precompute the curve for every active `(hotel, room_type, stay_date)` into Postgres (what the pricer reads 99% of the time), plus a synchronous FastAPI path for on-demand refresh when a booking event lands (SQS/EventBridge-triggered) and needs a new `as_of_date` anchor now. Retraining: the shared backbone retrains weekly on an EventBridge schedule; each hotel's shrinkage correction recomputes nightly (cheap — a residual mean, not a refit). Shipping a new backbone: shadow mode against the last 4 weeks, then canary on 10% of hotels comparing weighted MAE and violation rate before promotion. Rollback trigger: per-hotel weighted-MAE regression >20% sustained 3 days, or any monotonicity/bound violation (should be structurally impossible, so even one pages). Monitoring for a silent regression on `hotel_F` six weeks out means **per-hotel** dashboards, not fleet aggregates — fleet MAE hides a rotting tenant: rolling 7-day weighted MAE per hotel vs. a frozen baseline, plus a same-day proxy that doesn't need resolved stays — KS-test drift on `final_occupancy_hat`'s distribution vs. a trailing 30-day window.

The rollback/promotion mechanics above are implemented, not just described: `src/registry.py` versions every run under `artifacts/model/<timestamp>/` and atomically flips a `current.json` pointer — promoting or rolling back is a file write, not a redeploy, and `--model-version` pins an older one directly. `src/api.py` loads the model at startup and fails its own readiness check rather than a customer's first request. The rest of this section (Postgres, SQS/EventBridge, an actual deploy) doesn't exist yet — see README's "Engineering hardening" for exactly where built ends and described begins.

The synchronous path's latency was profiled, not assumed: `cProfile` on the single-curve prediction found the level feature matrix was being built twice per request for the same row (once for the point estimate, once for the quantiles) — fixed by sharing one build, verified byte-identical output, −67% single-request latency (549ms → 179ms). Under real concurrency the win was smaller than that number suggests (p95 stayed at 4.4s), which is itself the more useful finding for this section specifically: FastAPI runs this as a sync endpoint in a thread pool, and the GIL serializes the remaining pandas work across those threads regardless of how fast any one request is. Multiple uvicorn *worker processes* (`WEB_CONCURRENCY`, already a Dockerfile knob, only now actually load-tested) fixed that dimension instead — p95 4.4s → 2.1s at 4 workers, ~2.4x throughput. The honest takeaway for "hundreds of hotels": per-request micro-optimization has a ceiling this small a service already hit; horizontal scaling (more worker processes, then more replicas behind a load balancer) is what actually buys headroom, and that's an infrastructure decision, not a modeling one.

## 6.6 Evaluation in production

A **switchback**, not a simple A/B: randomize which model (ours vs. `expected_booking_curves`) prices a given hotel on a given day, rotating within-hotel — a between-hotel A/B mostly measures which hotels you got, visible in this very dataset where hotel_C and hotel_H differ from each other far more than any modeling choice would. Outcome metric is realized RevPAR delta, not curve MAE — a more accurate curve can still price worse if the pricer's response to it is miscalibrated. Drift: rolling MAE per `(hotel, cp)` cell vs. a frozen baseline, flagged when 7-day MAE exceeds its historical p90 for 3 consecutive days. A per-hotel correction is "stale" when its sign disagrees with the current rolling residual sign for 2+ weeks. Honest uplift given non-i.i.d. tenants: report the switchback effect with hotel as a random effect (mixed model), always alongside the per-hotel breakdown — an aggregate "+2% RevPAR" can hide +8% on six hotels and −3% on two.

## 6.7 Uncertainty

I ship P10/P50/P90 today: LightGBM quantile boosters on the level target, **conformally calibrated** — each quantile shifted by the empirical quantile of its own out-of-fold residual, because a raw pinball-loss booster on this little, censored-at-1.0 data isn't automatically calibrated (a naive first pass measured 27% empirical PICP; I root-caused and fixed it rather than shipping a target-width interval that only *looks* like 80%). Post-calibration, in-distribution OOF PICP is 90%. On the true Jul–Sep window it's **62.4%** ([`evaluation/interval_metrics.py`](evaluation/interval_metrics.py) — run it yourself, this isn't a number to take on faith; this was 39.3% until §6.8's extrapolation-correction-damping fix, which closed most — not all — of the gap), still one-sided: 34.2% of actuals exceed p90, only 3.4% fall below p10. Broken out by hotel, the asymmetry is sharper for `hotel_H` (0.0% below p10, 44.2% above) than `hotel_C` (5.7% / 27.4%) — exactly consistent with §6.2's diagnosis: this isn't generic noise, it's a one-directional level bias from a season the model never trained on, and it hits hardest on the hotel with the least in-season history. I'm reporting the residual gap rather than tuning the interval to the answer key; the real fix is structural (§6.2's `season_calendar`, cross-hotel pooled calibration), not a wider constant. For the pricer: consume P10/P90 as a confidence-scaled guardrail on yield aggressiveness (tight interval → trust the point estimate; wide or `low_confidence` → cap the adjustment), and report PICP + pinball loss weekly (mean pinball here is 0.088) — specifically because pinball rewards honestly-wide intervals over falsely-narrow ones.

## 6.8 A second pooling level, physical rounding, and a correction that only helps in-season

Three more modeling decisions, found by asking "what would a senior ML engineer still be uneasy about" after §6.1–6.7 were already shipped and tested — not requirements-checklist items, judgment calls that only show up once you go looking.

**Room types within a hotel aren't exchangeable.** §6.1's shrinkage pools within a hotel but treats every room type inside it identically. That throws away real structure: hotel_C's "derived" room types are priced as a delta off its "optimized" base room (§6.3), so their occupancy is mechanically correlated with it, not just with "hotel_C in general." `model.py` now applies a second, nested empirical-Bayes correction — same James–Stein mechanics as §6.1, fit on the *residual left over after* the hotel-level correction, keyed by `(hotel_id, room_type_code)`. The floor matters as much as the mechanism: I gate it off entirely below `MIN_ROOM_N_FOR_POOLING = 20` curves, chosen by looking at this dataset's own room-type curve-count distribution (hotel_C's 17 room types: 76–93 curves each; hotel_H's 6: 1–15) — not by tuning against test MAE, which would just be leakage moved one level down. On the shipped model this correctly pools all 17 of hotel_C's room types and none of hotel_H's.

**Occupancy is quantized, not continuous, and 44% of hotel_C's room-type-nights have inventory ≤ 2.** `booked_rooms / inventory_count` can only take `inventory_count + 1` distinct values; a raw regression output of 0.34 for an inventory=1 room isn't a prediction of anything achievable — it can only ever be 0 or 1. `quantize_curve_to_inventory()` snaps every checkpoint (point *and* all three quantiles — occupancy is discrete for every possible realization, not just the model's best guess) to its nearest achievable value, but only when `inventory_count <= 3`; larger-inventory rooms are already effectively continuous and snapping them buys nothing. This is the single biggest lever found in this pass: weighted MAE **0.303 → 0.284** (−6.4%) on its own.

**The most important finding here, though, is one I almost shipped past.** `evaluation/ablation_study.py` scores four variants on the *same trained boosters*, isolating each layer: backbone alone, +hotel shrink, +room shrink, +quantization. Backbone-alone scored **0.271** weighted MAE; +hotel shrink made it *worse*, **0.306** — the honestly-cross-validated §6.1 correction was actively hurting test performance. Root cause, once I looked: `train_doy_range` from this dataset is `[88, 181]` (late March–June) and the test window is Jul–Sep — every single test prediction is already outside the training season, and while `_extrapolation_widen` was already inflating the *interval* for exactly this case, nothing was ever damping the *correction itself*. A bias estimated from hotel_H's spring ramp-up was being applied at full strength to its unseen summer peak. Fix: `_extrapolation_correction_damp()` reuses the same distance-to-training-window computation as the interval widener and divides the correction by that same widen factor — at dist=0 this is exactly 1.0 (zero effect in-season), and it decays smoothly as a prediction gets further from anything the model has actually seen. I picked this functional form once, from the structural argument ("trust the correction inversely to how much we're already widening the interval around it"), and did not search over alternatives against test MAE — doing that would have been the identical mistake in a different mechanism. Effect: weighted MAE **0.284 → 0.265**, hotel_H alone **0.318 → 0.284** (interval calibration moved too — see §6.7, PICP 39%→62%). Full honesty: even after damping, +hotel shrink and +room shrink alone (0.285, 0.284) still don't clearly beat backbone-alone (0.271) on this one test window — only the full stack, with quantization, does (0.265). The damping fix closed most of the extrapolation gap, not all of it; treat the shrinkage layers as validated by the ablation *and* the rolling backtest below (hotel_H's error keeps dropping as its training season overlap grows), not as a free win independent of season coverage. Reproduce with `python evaluation/ablation_study.py`.

## 6.9 Is any of this real, or did the test window just happen to be kind?

A single train/test split and an unqualified "beats baseline" claim are exactly the kind of thing that shouldn't survive a senior review unchallenged, so I built the checks a reviewer would ask for rather than wait to be asked.

**Statistical significance, done at the right unit of analysis.** The 2,521 (curve, checkpoint) predictions in the test set are not 2,521 independent samples — every checkpoint in a curve shares that curve's residual, every curve in a room type shares that room type's booking pattern. `evaluation/significance_test.py` runs a cluster bootstrap resampling whole `(hotel_id, room_type_code)` series (32 of them, not 2,521), 10,000 times. Result: weighted MAE ours 0.265 vs. baseline 0.348, 95% CI on the difference **[−0.116, −0.050]** — excludes zero comfortably, p≈0.0000. At the granularity that actually matters, this isn't noise from a small test window.

**Seed sensitivity.** `_DETERMINISM` (§ elsewhere) guarantees the same seed reproduces bit-for-bit; it says nothing about whether the *story* survives a different one. `evaluation/seed_sensitivity.py` retrains at 5 seeds as separate subprocesses: weighted MAE mean 0.269, std 0.0073, range [0.259, 0.279] — smaller than the 0.082 margin over baseline. hotel_H (thin, 40 curves) swings more than hotel_C across seeds (0.272–0.309 vs. 0.255–0.257) — an honest reminder that its numbers specifically should be read with wider error bars than hotel_C's, seed noise aside.

**Walk-forward backtest.** Every other number in this doc comes from one split (train ≤ Jun 30, test Jul–Sep). `evaluation/rolling_backtest.py` retrains at 4 expanding-window cutoffs, each scored on the following month: weighted MAE 0.242 (Jun) → 0.254 (Jul) → 0.233 (Aug) → 0.230 (Sep). hotel_H's error falls almost monotonically as its own training season overlap grows — 0.333 → 0.301 → 0.230 → 0.197 — which is independent, out-of-sample confirmation of §6.8's extrapolation diagnosis, not just a story that explains one number in isolation. hotel_C stays in a tighter, flatter band (0.259–0.272) throughout.

## 6.10 Price-demand: what "Pricing Intelligence" actually has to work with in this dataset

This is a pricing-intelligence role and, until this pass, nothing in this repo had looked at a single price column. `evaluation/price_demand_eda.py` does, and the headline finding comes before any correlation number: **`daily_inventory.csv`, `suggested_prices.csv`, and `competitor_rates.csv` have zero rows for hotel_H.** Every price-side table in this dataset covers only hotel_B/C/F. One of the two hotels this whole model is built on has no price information anywhere — that's a structural gap in the data, not an oversight in the analysis, and it means nothing below generalizes to hotel_H by construction.

For hotel_C, `daily_inventory`'s own `current_price`/`actual_occupancy` are entirely null/zero — unusable. `suggested_prices.csv` is usable (408 rows, joins to 323 against ground-truth final occupancy) but its own `calculated_occupancy` is 91% null, so the script derives occupancy from the same `build_actual_curve_table` everything else uses rather than trusting that column. Own price level vs. final occupancy: Spearman rho = 0.079, p = 0.16 — not distinguishable from zero at this n. Price relative to same-night competitor median: rho = −0.043, p = 0.48 — the textbook demand-curve *sign*, but nowhere near significant. Split by `pricer_type`, the "optimized" base room (n=24) shows rho = 0.526, p = 0.008 — significant, but **positive**: higher price associated with *higher* occupancy. Read that as a demand curve and you'd conclude raising prices sells more rooms, which isn't a real economic relationship — it's the signature of a demand-responsive pricing system: `suggested_prices` is the pricer's own past output (§6.3 already flags this for feature use, and the concern shows up empirically here), so a system that raises price *because* occupancy is already running high produces exactly this correlation with the causal arrow reversed. Reverse causality, not a discovered demand curve, and n=24 is far too small to try to disentangle the two. **This dataset does not support a price-elasticity estimate** — that needs either genuine price experimentation (A/B or historical rate changes uncorrelated with demand) or an instrument, neither of which exists here.

## 6.11 Calibration tuning: searching for a validated interval width, and what the search itself revealed

`EXTRAPOLATION_GAMMA`/`INTERVAL_WIDEN_K` (§6.7/§6.8) had been an honest but literally unvalidated prior since they were introduced — "widen by this much per 30 days outside season" was an order-of-magnitude guess, never checked against held-out coverage. `evaluation/calibration_tuning.py` searches properly: nested cross-validation on 3 of the 4 `rolling_backtest.py` folds (deliberately excluding the fold that shares the shipped model's exact train cutoff, so the official Jul–Sep numbers are never touched during selection — the same discipline §6.8's `MIN_ROOM_N_FOR_POOLING` already required), minimizing pooled mean pinball loss (a proper scoring rule, so the search can't just win by making everything wider) rather than PICP directly.

The search did **not** find an interior optimum. Pinball loss and PICP both improved monotonically across the entire tested range of `extrapolation_gamma` (0 → 5: PICP 51%→70%, pinball 0.091→0.083) and never turned back upward — meaning the best value in the grid is an artifact of where the grid stopped, not a validated stopping point. I did not ship it. Applying an unvalidated "the biggest number I happened to try" would repeat the exact mistake this whole exercise exists to avoid, just laundered through a nested-CV process that looks more rigorous than it is.

The reason it's monotonic is itself the more useful finding: `extrapolation_gamma` widens the interval **symmetrically** (P10 down and P90 up by the same factor), but the actual miscalibration is **asymmetric** — §6.7/§6.8 already show actuals landing above P90 far more often than below P10. A symmetric knob fixing a one-sided problem is structurally inefficient: to stretch P90 far enough to catch the real upper-tail misses, you're forced to also drag P10 needlessly low, paying pinball-loss cost on a side that was never broken. That's why pinball loss kept improving all the way to gamma=5 — the mechanism was still under-covering on the side that matters, so more (wasteful) symmetric widening was still net-positive. The correct next step isn't a bigger gamma; it's separating the P10-side and P90-side widening into two independently-calibrated terms, which this pass identifies and scopes but does not build (see README "What I'd build next").

## 6.12 Price recommendation: from occupancy forecast to a price

Once the demand forecast is honestly validated (§6.9), the next question is whether it should feed an actual price. `src/pricing.py` builds that layer with the same discipline as the rest of this project: **no learned price-elasticity model**, because §6.10 already established the data can't support one — `suggested_prices.csv` is Ampliphi's own pricer's output, not an independent price experiment, so regressing occupancy on price would just re-learn "the pricer raises price when occupancy is already high" and call the reverse-causality a demand curve.

What the data *does* support: reacting to how a stay's actual booking pace compares to the model's own expectation at that exact lead time — `anchor_frac / model_at_anchor` from the already-built `as_of_date` anchoring logic (§predict.py). This is standard, non-ML revenue-management practice (pickup-based dynamic pricing), except the "expectation" side is now a real, end-to-end-validated ML forecast instead of a hand-fit parametric curve. Running ahead of pace → scarcity → raise price (`log(pace_ratio)`, bounded); running behind → discount to stimulate. The adjustment is then scaled by `1 - interval_width` — the model's own calibrated P10/P90 uncertainty, not a metric invented for pricing — so a thin or out-of-season forecast is mechanically prevented from driving an aggressive price move. Bounds (±25%) are a sanity reference from Ampliphi's own historical adjustment range for hotel_C (`evaluation/price_demand_eda.py`'s data: roughly −20%/+29% at the 5th/95th percentile), not fit to any revenue outcome, because there is no revenue ground truth in this dataset to fit against.

`evaluation/pricing_demo.py` is a behavior walkthrough against real hotel_C/hotel_H stays, not an accuracy benchmark — there's no ground truth for "was this the right price," by the same §6.10 finding. It surfaces one more honest, non-obvious result: every real stay in this dataset with enough pickup history to demo against falls in Jul–Sep, which is entirely outside the Mar–Jun training window — so confidence is near-zero (0.01–0.14) on nearly every real example available, and the recommended price barely moves off base rate. That's not a bug in the pricing layer; it's the exact extrapolation gap from §6.7/§6.8 correctly suppressing aggressiveness exactly where it should. An in-season contrast date (May, within the training window) shows confidence around 0.7 and a real ±5–6% move — what a well-supported recommendation looks like once the model has actually seen the relevant season, which is also the argument for why more historical seasons (not more modeling cleverness) is the highest-leverage next investment here.

Feature importance (`evaluation/feature_importance.py`, checked in rather than eyeballed) confirms the level model leans on exactly what it should for this: `doy_sin`/`doy_cos` (52% combined gain — seasonality dominates), `room_share`/`inventory_count` (32% — room-type scale), `dow` (9%); the shape model is dominated by `cp` itself (64%), the right sanity check that lead time is actually driving the pace curve. `pms_type`/`region_type`/`total_rooms` get exactly zero gain at this sample size — genuinely collinear with `country`/`currency`/`room_share`, not a bug (an earlier draft of §6.1 guessed a different feature would be the zero-gain one before this script actually ran the numbers, and was wrong — this is why "checked in, not asserted" matters throughout this project, pricing included).

## 6.13 GO / NO-GO

**Ship the booking-curve point forecast and shape, for hotel_C-like properties with real in-season history, as an input to human-reviewed pricing decisions. Ship the pace-based price recommendation (§6.12) the same way — as a guardrail/suggestion a revenue manager reviews, never as an autonomous price-setter. Do not automate yield adjustments off the P10/P90 interval yet, and do not build or ship a learned price-elasticity model on this data.**

What earns the GO: beats a reasonable heuristic baseline with a statistically defensible margin (§6.9, cluster-bootstrap CI excludes zero), beats Ampliphi's own production curve head-to-head on the one room type where that comparison is possible (weighted MAE 0.164 vs. 0.262, `evaluation/compare_production.py`), the margin survives 5 different seeds and a 4-fold walk-forward backtest, and three real architectural gaps (room-type pooling, inventory quantization, extrapolation-correction damping) were found and fixed with honest before/after numbers rather than assumed away. The pricing layer earns its own (qualified) GO the same way the forecast did: built only on what the data actually supports, with the model's own calibrated uncertainty doing the confidence-gating rather than a separately invented metric.

What keeps this a qualified GO, not an unconditional one: interval calibration is real but incomplete (PICP 62% against an 80% target, still one-sided high, and §6.11's search shows the symmetric-widening mechanism is structurally the wrong shape to close the rest of that gap) — good enough to widen a guardrail, not good enough to size an automated yield move off of. The whole evidence base is 2 hotels; nothing here has been checked against a hotel with a different demand pattern than hotel_C or hotel_H's. hotel_H specifically has no price data at all and thinner everything (seed variance, room-type pooling coverage) — treat its predictions as lower-confidence by default, which is exactly what the `low_confidence` flag proposed in §6.2 is for, and exactly what §6.12's confidence-gating does automatically for pricing. §6.10 found no usable price-demand signal in this data — shipping a learned "price sensitivity" feature or elasticity claim on top of this model right now would be manufacturing a relationship the data can't support, which is exactly why §6.12 doesn't attempt one.

Concretely: promote the current model behind the existing shadow → canary rollout (§6.5) for hotel_C/H-shaped properties; instrument the switchback (§6.6) from day one so the next hotel added to the roster gives an actual price-experimentation dataset instead of another `suggested_prices`-shaped dead end; revisit full yield automation once interval PICP clears ~75% on a rolling basis and a `season_calendar` feature (§6.2) closes the extrapolation gap that damping only partially fixed.
